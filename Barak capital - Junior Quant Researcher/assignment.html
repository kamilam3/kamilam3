Home Assignment: Exchange Delay Prediction

Quantitative Trading — Latency Analysis

01 Overview

Background

In high-frequency trading, the network delay between an exchange and our servers is not constant — it fluctuates over time and can shift between distinct regimes. Understanding and anticipating these shifts is operationally valuable: if we know that delays are about to spike, we can adjust quoting strategies, widen risk thresholds, or temporarily reduce exposure.

Many factors influence network delay. The exchange’s internal matching engine processes messages at varying speeds depending on its load — during periods of intense trading activity (e.g., around economic announcements or large order bursts), the exchange may queue messages, inflating delay. Network infrastructure between the exchange and our co-located servers introduces its own variability: congestion on shared network paths, switch buffering, and even time-of-day patterns in overall market traffic can all leave fingerprints in the delay distribution. Finally, correlated products often share infrastructure at the exchange side, so a delay spike on one channel can be an early warning signal for a spike on a related channel shortly after.

The Task

You are given historical message logs from the Narnia Stock Exchange (NSE) for two correlated products: Stock_A and Stock_B. Each product is published on a separate exchange channel.

At each message arrival, your model must answer: “If a message arrived right now, what would its delay be?” More precisely, for each message at position i, predict the numeric delay in nanoseconds of that message — using only information from messages 0 through i−1 (and cross-channel data up to the current time).

03 Prediction Task

For each channel independently, at every message position i, predict the delay of message i in nanoseconds. At the time of prediction, you know the arrival_time and message_type of message i (the message has arrived at your server), but you do not know its sending_time — that is what you are estimating. Your prediction for message i may use all columns of messages 0 through i−1 on the same channel, plus any messages from the other channel with arrival_time < arrival_time of message i on the same channel.

Constraints

  1. Online only. Your model must be causal — at position i, you may only use data from messages 0 through i−1 (no look-ahead).
  2. Two separate predictions. You must produce a prediction stream for Stock_A and a prediction stream for Stock_B. Cross-channel information is allowed.
  3. Language. You may use any programming language and any libraries or frameworks you wish.
  4. No external data. You may not use any data beyond the provided CSV files.
  5. Warm-up period. The first 1,000 messages of each day will not be evaluated. You may output any prediction for these rows — use them to initialize your model.

04 Evaluation

Your model will be evaluated on hidden test dates (same exchange, same products, different days). The test data has similar characteristics to the training days but is not identical.

Scoring

Predictions are scored using Mean Squared Error (MSE) between your predicted delay and the actual delay, both in nanoseconds:

MSE  =  &frac1n  ∑  (predicted_delayi − actual_delayi)²     (lower is better)

Where actual_delayi = arrival_timei − sending_timei and the sum runs over all evaluated messages (excluding the first 1,000 warm-up messages per day).

We will also report RMSE (root mean squared error) for interpretability — RMSE is in the same nanosecond units as the delay itself, making it easier to judge how close your predictions are in absolute terms.

06 Technical Notes

Important — Do Not Open CSV Files in Excel

The timestamp columns (sending_time, arrival_time) are int64 nanosecond values (19 digits). Excel silently converts large integers to float64, which only has ~15.9 significant digits — this truncates the last 3–4 digits and will corrupt your delay calculations.

Use Python / pandas, a text editor, or any tool that preserves int64 to inspect the files.

Note on Timestamp Resolution

You may notice that the sending_time field has its last 3 digits as 000. This is an artifact of the recording pipeline, not a bug. The arrival_time field has full nanosecond resolution. The delay (arrival_time − sending_time) is accurate to within ±500 ns, which is negligible for this task.
